About Time Learning

Hear from the people inside

About Time is written by people in prison. This section helps teachers and students use that writing to understand incarceration in Australia, in their own words.

Why this section exists

  • Most Australians have never been inside a prison. What they know comes mainly from news and entertainment.
  • That coverage rarely includes the voices of people in prison themselves.
  • Reading first-person accounts helps people see individuals rather than labels.
  • About Time already publishes this writing every month. This section makes it easy to use in classrooms.
Content note. Some pieces discuss violence, grief, self-harm and deaths in custody. Every resource is labelled so teachers and students can choose what to read.
Acknowledgement of Country. We acknowledge the Traditional Custodians of the Lands on which About Time is written and read, and recognise their continuing connection to Country and culture. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are imprisoned at far higher rates than other Australians, a result of systemic factors including the ongoing impacts of colonisation. Resources featuring First Nations writers are developed with advice from First Nations people.

For Teachers

Free Reading Collections built from About Time's published writing. Each collection is a set of pieces with teaching guides for different subjects and year levels, mapped to the NSW syllabus.

How the collections work

1. Choose a collection

Each collection groups 4 to 5 pieces by people in prison around one theme, plus an alternative piece for students who opt out.

2. Choose a teaching guide

Each collection has guides for different subjects and stages. The pieces stay the same; the questions, activities and syllabus links change.

3. Print or save

Every guide can be printed or saved as a PDF. Read the content warnings and the safe teaching guide before you start.

Teaching this topic safely

  • Students may have family in prison. Never ask students to share personal or family connections to the justice system.
  • Give content warnings before each reading and offer the alternative piece listed in each collection.
  • Acknowledge victims. These pieces share the perspective of people in prison, not the whole story. Make space for that in discussion. Support: Victims Services NSW.
  • Avoid cultural load. Do not ask First Nations students to explain or speak for their communities.
  • Check your school's procedures for teaching controversial issues, including principal approval and parent notification.
  • Wellbeing support: school counsellor; Kids Helpline 1800 55 1800.

Teaching guides

2 lessons · 60 minutes eachPieces 1, 2, 3 and poem; Piece 4 for Year 10 at teacher discretion

Learning intention

Students evaluate how first-person writing represents people in prison, and compare this with news reporting.

Success criteria

  • I can identify language choices that shape how a writer wants to be seen.
  • I can compare how two texts represent people in prison.
  • I can write a response for a real audience, controlling my voice and language.

Lesson sequence

  1. Lesson 1, 10 min: Students write anonymously what they picture when they hear "prison". Keep the notes for Lesson 2.
  2. Lesson 1, 25 min: In pairs, read Piece 1 and the poem. Annotate words and details that show how each writer wants to be seen.
  3. Lesson 1, 25 min: "Compare the coverage" task using a news report the teacher selects.
  4. Lesson 2, 25 min: Read Pieces 2 and 3. Discuss the questions below, then revisit the Lesson 1 notes: what has changed?
  5. Lesson 2, 35 min: Draft a Letter from Outside.

Discussion questions

  1. What details does the writer choose to include about their day? What might they have left out, and why?
  2. How does the writer want you to see them? Point to specific words or phrases.
  3. How does the poem's form create a different effect from the prose pieces?
  4. How does the writer's voice (first person, tone, humour) shape your response?
  5. How does the news report represent people in prison differently from these pieces?
  6. Whose perspectives are missing from these pieces? Why might that matter?

Activities

Compare the coverage

Complete a table for an About Time piece and a news report: who speaks, what words describe people in prison, what the reader is invited to feel. Then write one paragraph evaluating which text is more persuasive and why.

Letter from Outside (assessment option)

Write a 200 to 300 word letter responding to one piece. Criteria: responds to specific details in the piece; voice suits a real reader; language is controlled and respectful. Selected letters can be submitted through the Letters from Outside page.

Differentiation

  • Support: read pieces aloud; provide sentence starters for the letter ("When you wrote ... I noticed ...").
  • Extension: Year 10 students compare Piece 4 with a news article on homelessness and evaluate each text's argument.

Discussion questions

  1. How does Donna use ordinary, concrete details to make a reader see daily life inside?
  2. How does the anonymous writer of Piece 3 structure a reflection on time? How does the ending shift the tone?
  3. What does Martin's poem achieve through form that prose could not?

Activity: model and compose

  2. Compose: write a 500 to 700 word reflective piece about a place or routine that shapes your own sense of time. Borrow one technique from the model.

Students write about their own experience of time and routine, not about prison or anyone else's experience of it.

Reading Collection 2: First Nations Voices

How do Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in prison keep culture, family and advocacy strong, in their own words?

Before you teach.
  • Talk to your school's Aboriginal Education Officer or local Aboriginal Education Consultative Group (AECG) before using this collection.
  • Refer to writers by the Nation and terms they use for themselves, and follow the Narragunnawali terminology guide.
  • Avoid cultural load: do not ask First Nations students to explain or speak for their communities.
  • Keep a strengths-based focus. These pieces are about culture, art, family, education and advocacy, as well as the impacts of incarceration.
  • Check each piece for names or images of people who have died, and give a warning if needed.

How to use these pieces in Legal Studies

Lived accounts are perspectives, not legal evidence. Pair them with legal and official sources: the Royal Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in Custody (1991), the Australian Law Reform Commission's Pathways to Justice (Report 133, 2017), and ABS Prisoners in Australia.

Discussion questions

  1. What do Pieces 1 and 2 suggest culturally appropriate programs inside prison can look like?
  2. How does KC's account of history help explain overrepresentation today?
  3. Which recommendations in Pathways to Justice respond to issues the writers raise?
  4. To what extent does the criminal justice system achieve justice for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples?

Activity: extended response plan

Plan a response to question 4 using five parts: claim; legal source; statistic; lived perspective from a piece; judgement.

Prison in Australia: the facts

46,998adults in prison at 30 June 2025, up 6% in a year
37%of people in prison were Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, compared with about 3.8% of the population
42%were on remand (not yet sentenced), up from 31% in 2016
60%had been in adult prison before

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Prisoners in Australia, 2025; ABS 2021 Census. Updated each year when new data is released.

Why are Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people overrepresented in prison?

Major inquiries have linked overrepresentation to systemic factors, including the ongoing impacts of colonisation, over-policing and unequal access to services, rather than individual choices alone. Start with:

  • Royal Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in Custody (1991)
  • Australian Law Reform Commission, Pathways to Justice (Report 133, 2017)
  • National Agreement on Closing the Gap, Targets 10 (adults) and 11 (children aged 10 to 17)

Words matter

The words we use shape how people are seen. About Time uses language that describes a situation, not a whole person.

Using About Time in your work

How to cite

APA 7:
Writer, A. (Year, Month). Title of article. About Time. URL

Harvard:
Writer, A. (Year) 'Title of article', About Time, Month. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

If a writer uses a first name or pen name only, cite it as published.

Read critically

  • Each piece is one person's perspective, not everyone's.
  • Some claims can't be independently checked. Pair them with official data.
  • Ask what the writer chooses to include, and what they might leave out.
  • Notice how voice and language shape your response.

Act ethically

  • Don't try to contact or identify writers.
  • Quote only what's published.
  • Represent writers fairly, including the parts of their story that complicate your argument.
